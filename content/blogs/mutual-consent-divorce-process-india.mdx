# Mutual Consent Divorce in India: Process, Timeline and Real Costs

Most people searching for this are not looking for legal theory. They want to know three things: how long it takes, what it costs, and what they have to agree on before they walk into a courtroom.

This guide covers the process as it actually runs. It is written from the coaching side of the table — what couples find difficult, where the delays really come from, and which decisions cause the most damage when they are rushed. It is not legal advice, and you should retain a lawyer before filing anything.

## Key Takeaways

- Mutual consent divorce requires both spouses to agree, and to have lived separately for at least one year before filing.
- The process runs in two stages, called the first and second motion, separated by a statutory cooling-off period of six months.
- Since 2017 the Supreme Court has allowed courts to waive that six-month wait where reconciliation is genuinely impossible, which is why some divorces conclude in three to four months.
- Court fees are minor; lawyer fees are the real cost and vary enormously by city and by how much you have already agreed between yourselves.
- The settlement — money, property, custody — is where the time actually goes. Couples who negotiate it before filing move fastest.

## What Mutual Consent Divorce Means

A mutual consent divorce is one where both spouses agree to end the marriage and agree on the terms of ending it. Nobody has to prove cruelty, desertion or adultery. Nobody has to be found at fault.

The provision depends on which law your marriage falls under:

- **Section 13B, Hindu Marriage Act 1955** — for Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist and Jain marriages.
- **Section 28, Special Marriage Act 1954** — for civil marriages, including most interfaith marriages.
- **Section 10A, Indian Divorce Act 1869** — for Christian marriages.
- **Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act 1936** — for Parsi marriages.
- Under Muslim personal law, *khula* and *mubarat* provide routes to dissolution by consent, and the procedure differs from the statutes above.

The practical requirements under Section 13B are straightforward: the couple must have been living separately for at least one year, must be unable to live together, and must both agree the marriage should end.

"Living separately" is worth clarifying, because it causes needless anxiety. Courts have consistently read it as living separately *as spouses*, not necessarily at separate addresses. Couples who continued sharing a flat for financial or family reasons while the marriage was effectively over have satisfied this requirement. What matters is the absence of a marital relationship, not the absence of a shared postcode.

## The Two-Motion Process, Step by Step

### Step 1: The joint petition

Both spouses file a joint petition in the family court that has jurisdiction — usually where the marriage was solemnised, where you last lived together, or where the wife currently resides.

The petition sets out the marriage details, the period of separation, the confirmation that reconciliation has failed, and the agreed terms of settlement.

### Step 2: First motion

Both parties appear before the court. Statements are recorded confirming that the consent is genuine and not obtained by coercion. The court satisfies itself that both people understand what they are signing.

Once the first motion is recorded, the statutory waiting period begins.

### Step 3: The cooling-off period

Section 13B(2) provides a gap of not less than six months and not more than eighteen months between the two motions. The stated purpose is to give couples an opportunity to reconsider.

This is the part most people find hardest, and it is also the part that has changed most in recent years — covered in the next section.

### Step 4: Second motion

After the waiting period, both parties appear again and confirm that they still want the divorce. If either spouse withdraws consent at this stage, the mutual consent petition fails. It cannot be forced through; that is the entire nature of a consent-based divorce.

### Step 5: Decree

Satisfied that consent is intact and the settlement is fair, the court passes a decree of divorce. The marriage ends on the date of the decree.

## The Cooling-Off Period Can Be Waived

This is the single most useful thing to know, and the thing most people are not told.

In **Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur (2017)**, the Supreme Court held that the six-month period under Section 13B(2) is directory rather than mandatory. Family courts may waive it where the couple has already been separated for a substantial period, where all issues including maintenance and custody are genuinely settled, and where the waiting period would only prolong distress with no prospect of reconciliation.

The Supreme Court went further in **Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan (2023)**, confirming it can dissolve a marriage directly under Article 142 of the Constitution where the marriage has irretrievably broken down — bypassing the waiting period entirely in appropriate cases.

Two practical points follow from this:

**A waiver is applied for, not automatic.** Your lawyer files a separate application setting out why the period should be waived. Family courts vary considerably in how readily they grant it.

**The strength of your settlement matters.** A waiver application succeeds far more often when maintenance, property and custody are already fully resolved and documented, because the court can see there is nothing left to reconsider.

## How Long It Actually Takes

With the full cooling-off period, expect roughly **eight to twelve months** from filing to decree in most family courts. The statutory six months is a floor, not a schedule — court listings, adjournments and workload add to it.

With a successful waiver, **three to five months** is realistic.

Delays, when they happen, are rarely legal. In practice they come from:

- **An unsettled agreement.** Couples who file before agreeing terms end up negotiating between motions, and each unresolved item adds hearings.
- **One party leaving the country.** Both must appear. Video appearance is permitted in many courts but needs to be arranged in advance.
- **Late second thoughts.** Entirely legitimate, and the reason the waiting period exists — but it resets everything.
- **Court backlog**, which varies enormously between cities and even between benches.

## What It Costs

**Court fees** are small — typically a few hundred to a few thousand rupees depending on the state.

**Lawyer fees** are the actual cost, and the spread is wide. A straightforward uncontested mutual divorce handled by a local practitioner may run from around ₹25,000. In metro cities, with a senior lawyer and a complex settlement involving property, fees well above ₹1,50,000 are common.

Two things reduce cost more than anything else:

**Agree the settlement before you instruct lawyers.** Every point you resolve between yourselves is a point your lawyers are not billing hours to negotiate. This is where couples save the most money and it is almost entirely within your control.

**Consider whether one lawyer can file for both.** In a genuinely amicable mutual divorce, some couples use a single lawyer to draft and file the joint petition. This is common practice and considerably cheaper. It is only appropriate where there is no meaningful conflict of interest and both people understand the terms fully — if there is any imbalance in information or bargaining power, take separate advice.

**Free legal aid** is available through District Legal Services Authorities for those who qualify on income grounds, and the National Legal Services Authority helpline can direct you.

## The Settlement Is the Real Work

The court process is procedural. The difficult part is deciding what you are each walking away with, and that happens before any of it.

Four things need to be resolved:

**Maintenance or alimony.** A lump sum, monthly payments, or an explicit waiver by both parties. Whatever you decide, it should be stated clearly in the settlement, because a vague agreement is the most common source of post-divorce litigation.

**Property and assets.** The matrimonial home, jointly held accounts, investments, vehicles. Also worth addressing: who continues servicing a joint loan, and how the other person is released from liability.

**Streedhan and gifts.** Jewellery, gifts given at the time of marriage, and items belonging to the wife. This is one of the most frequently disputed categories and benefits enormously from being itemised in writing.

**Custody and maintenance of children.** Where they live, how the other parent sees them, who pays for what, and how education and medical costs are shared as they grow. Courts scrutinise this more closely than any other term, because the child's welfare is a separate consideration from what the parents have agreed.

## What Coaching Can and Cannot Do Here

A coach cannot file your petition or advise you on the law. What this process consistently produces, though, is a set of conversations that couples find nearly impossible to have alone — and those are workable.

Couples come to us mid-process for three things. First, negotiating the settlement without it collapsing into a fight, particularly where children are involved and both people know they will be in contact for the next two decades. Second, deciding whether the divorce is actually what they want, which the cooling-off period is designed to surface and which many couples spend six months avoiding. Third, handling the family and social fallout, which in Indian families is frequently harder than the legal process itself.

If reconciliation is genuinely on the table, that is worth exploring properly rather than by default. If it is not, an amicable separation is worth a great deal — financially, and for anyone who has to co-parent afterwards.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a mutual consent divorce be filed before one year of marriage?**
Not ordinarily. Section 14 of the Hindu Marriage Act bars divorce petitions within the first year of marriage, and Section 13B additionally requires one year of separation. Courts may permit an earlier petition in cases of exceptional hardship or depravity, but this requires a specific application and is not granted routinely.

**What happens if my spouse withdraws consent before the second motion?**
The mutual consent petition fails. Consent must exist at both motions, and a court cannot grant a mutual divorce where one party has withdrawn. Your remaining option is a contested divorce on statutory grounds, which is slower and considerably more expensive. This is precisely why the settlement should be complete and documented before the first motion.

**Do both spouses need to be physically present in court?**
Both must appear for the recording of statements. Many family courts now permit appearance by video conferencing where a party is abroad or unable to travel, but this needs to be applied for rather than assumed. Some courts also accept appearance through a duly authorised representative in limited circumstances. Confirm the position with your lawyer for the specific court.

**Is mutual consent divorce possible without a lawyer?**
Legally you may appear in person, but it is a poor idea. The settlement terms recorded in the decree determine your financial and parental obligations for years, and drafting errors are difficult to correct afterwards. If cost is the barrier, District Legal Services Authorities provide free legal aid to those who qualify.

**How is a mutual divorce different from a contested one?**
A mutual divorce requires no allegations and no evidence of fault — you are simply telling the court you both want it to end. A contested divorce requires one spouse to prove a statutory ground such as cruelty, desertion or adultery, involves witnesses and cross-examination, and commonly runs for several years. Where mutual consent is achievable, it is faster, cheaper and significantly less damaging.

**Can the settlement terms be changed after the decree?**
Financial terms recorded in a consent decree are difficult to reopen; courts treat them as a concluded agreement. Arrangements concerning children are treated differently — custody and child maintenance can be revisited if circumstances change materially, because the child's welfare is a continuing consideration rather than a settled bargain.
