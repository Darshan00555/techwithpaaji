# Am I Ready for Marriage? 9 Questions That Actually Predict It

The standard readiness checklist is mostly about circumstances. Are you financially stable, are you old enough, have you been together long enough, do the families approve.

Those things matter for the wedding. They predict very little about the marriage.

What does predict it is a different and less comfortable set of questions — about how you handle disagreement, whether you have seen this person under pressure, and whether you are marrying a person or an outcome. Below are the nine worth answering honestly, and the three reasons for marrying that reliably go wrong.

## Key Takeaways

- Financial stability and time together predict far less about marriage success than how a couple handles disagreement.
- Seeing someone under genuine stress is more informative than years of seeing them at their best.
- The most reliable warning sign is having a list of things you cannot raise with them.
- Marrying to resolve pressure, loneliness or a deadline predicts poor outcomes regardless of how suitable the person is.

---

## 1. Have You Seen Them Under Real Pressure?

Not inconvenience. Actual pressure — a bereavement, a job loss, a serious family illness, a financial shock, a period where things genuinely went wrong.

Everyone is pleasant when circumstances are pleasant. What you need to know is what appears when they are not: whether they turn toward you or withdraw, whether stress makes them cruel, whether they can accept help, whether they can be wrong under strain.

If you have never seen it, you do not have this information. That is not a reason to postpone indefinitely, but it is worth knowing what you are missing. Couples who dated entirely during a stable period sometimes discover in year two that they have never actually met each other.

## 2. Can You Both Repair After an Argument?

The frequency of conflict predicts remarkably little. What predicts a great deal is how conflict ends.

Ask yourself concretely: after your last significant disagreement, what happened? Did someone apologise specifically? Did you reach a resolution, or did it simply stop and go unmentioned? Did either of you change anything afterwards?

Couples who argue often and repair well do considerably better than couples who rarely argue and never resolve anything. If your disagreements consistently end by evaporating rather than concluding, that pattern will not improve after a wedding — there will just be more to disagree about.

## 3. Is There Anything You Cannot Say to Them?

Make the list. Most people can do this in under a minute, which is itself informative.

Things you have not mentioned because of how they would react. Opinions you have edited. A financial fact, a family situation, a preference about children, a doubt.

A short list is normal. A long one — or a list containing something structurally important, like whether you want children — means you are proposing to spend decades managing this person rather than being known by them. That is exhausting in a way that compounds.

## 4. Do You Agree on the Four Things That Cannot Be Compromised?

Most disagreements in a marriage are negotiable. Four generally are not, and each needs a specific answer rather than an assumption.

**Children** — whether, and roughly when. There is no half-position available here, and a mismatch discovered at thirty-four is a different problem from one discussed at twenty-eight.

**Where you will live** — city, country, and whether that includes living with parents.

**Money** — how much is shared, how much goes to extended family, and who decides above what threshold.

**Careers** — whose moves if only one can, and whether either of you is expected to step back later.

Vague agreement here is not agreement. If you cannot each state the other's position accurately, you have not had the conversation.

## 5. Are You Marrying a Person or an Outcome?

A useful test: describe what you like about them using only their own qualities. No reference to their family, their standing, what your parents think, or the life stage the marriage represents.

If very little survives that, the attachment may be to the outcome — being married, being settled, having the question resolved — rather than to this person specifically. That is extremely common and rarely admitted, and it tends to surface around year three, when the outcome has been achieved and only the person remains.

## 6. Have You Met Them Inside Their Family?

You are not marrying an individual. You are marrying a family system, and how someone behaves inside it is often quite different from how they behave with you.

Watch specifically: can they disagree with their parents? Do they defer entirely, or resist reflexively, or hold their own position calmly? How are decisions made, and who is expected to absorb the discomfort?

The single most reliable predictor of in-law conflict in Indian marriages is not the in-laws' temperament. It is whether your partner can hold a position with their own family. If they cannot, that job will fall to you, and you will lose.

## 7. Can You Be Bored Together?

Underrated, and quite diagnostic.

Most of a marriage is unremarkable. A long stretch of Tuesdays, a delayed train, an evening where neither of you has anything interesting to say. If the relationship only works when something is happening — travel, an occasion, a crisis — you have tested it under exactly the conditions that will be rarest.

Spend an ordinary weekend with no plans and see how it feels. Ease during an unstructured day is a better signal than intensity during a good one.

## 8. Are You Both Reasonably Settled in Yourself?

Not perfect. Not fully sorted. Settled enough that the marriage does not have a job to do.

A marriage undertaken to fix loneliness, provide direction, resolve a mental health difficulty or repair self-esteem is carrying a load it cannot carry. Those are all legitimate things to want addressed; a spouse is simply not the mechanism, and asking them to be produces a specific kind of disappointment.

The question is not whether you are content. It is whether you would be broadly all right if this did not happen.

## 9. Would You Choose This If Nobody Knew?

The final test, and the one that clears away most of the noise.

No wedding, no announcement, no family relief, no one impressed. Just the two of you and the ordinary life that follows.

If most of what you want survives that, the decision is about the relationship. If a substantial part evaporates, some of what you are responding to is the event and the resolution it provides, rather than the person you would be living with afterwards.

## Three Reasons That Reliably Do Not Work

**Because the pressure became unbearable.** Family pressure in India is real, sustained and genuinely exhausting. Marrying to end it works — the pressure stops immediately. It is replaced within a year by a different and more permanent pressure, and the person you married is now part of it.

**Because of an age deadline.** Thirty, or whatever number your family has fixed on. A deadline is a reason to start looking seriously. It is not information about a specific person, and it produces a predictable error: evaluating whether someone is acceptable rather than whether they are right.

**Because ending it would be difficult.** Long relationships develop enormous inertia — shared friends, families who have met, years invested, the awkwardness of explaining. None of that is a reason to marry someone. The cost of ending a long relationship is real and it is considerably lower than the cost of ending a marriage.

## What Does Not Predict Much

Worth stating, because these dominate the conversation and deserve less weight than they get.

**Age.** Beyond genuine youth, the number tells you little. Maturity varies enormously within any age bracket.

**Time together.** Couples who dated eight years divorce; couples who met through an arranged introduction and married in four months build stable marriages. What matters is what you learned in the time, not its length.

**Financial stability.** It reduces stress and it is worth having. It does not predict whether you can handle disagreement, which is the thing that actually determines the outcome.

**Family approval.** It makes life easier and it is a genuine advantage in an Indian context. It is not a substitute for compatibility, and plenty of well-approved marriages are unhappy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I know if I am ready for marriage or just under pressure?**
Separate the two by removing the pressure hypothetically. If your family were entirely content for you to stay unmarried another five years, would you still want to marry this person now? If the urgency disappears with the pressure, you are responding to the pressure. That does not necessarily mean the person is wrong — it means the timing is being decided by someone else.

**Is it a bad sign that I have doubts before marriage?**
Ordinary doubt about a large irreversible decision is normal and close to universal. What is worth examining is what the doubt is about. General anxiety about permanence is common. A specific recurring doubt about a particular thing — how they handle anger, whether you can be honest with them, whether they will side with you — is information rather than nerves, and it does not resolve after a wedding.

**How long should you date before getting married?**
There is no reliable number. What matters is whether you have seen each other under genuine stress, whether you have had the four non-negotiable conversations, and whether you have spent unstructured time together. Some couples cover all of that in eight months. Others avoid it for six years.

**Can you be ready for marriage but not for the right person?**
Yes, and it is a common and painful position. Readiness in yourself and compatibility with a specific person are separate questions. Being ready is a reason to look seriously; it is not a reason to proceed with whoever is currently available. The error it produces is treating your own readiness as evidence about them.

**What if we agree on everything except children?**
Then you disagree on the thing least amenable to compromise, because there is no partial version. Couples sometimes proceed hoping the other will change, which occasionally happens and more often produces deep resentment in whoever conceded. This is worth resolving properly before marrying rather than after, ideally with help, and the honest answer may be that you are not compatible despite everything else working.

**Should we do pre-marital counselling even if things are good?**
It is considerably more useful before problems exist than after. Its main function is not fixing anything — it is making sure both people have actually answered the same questions, which most couples assume they have and frequently have not. Four to six sessions, three to six months before the wedding, is the usual shape.

## The Short Version

If you strip this down: have you seen them at their worst, can you disagree and recover, is there anything you cannot say, and are you choosing this person or choosing to be married?

Everything else is detail.
