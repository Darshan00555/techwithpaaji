# Falling Out of Love: Is It a Phase or Is It Over?

The question almost always arrives the same way. Nothing is wrong. There was no betrayal, no terrible fight, no single event you can point at. The feeling has simply thinned, and you have started wondering whether its absence means something.

Here is the part nobody says clearly enough: the intense version of the feeling reliably fades in every long relationship. That is not a symptom. It is what happens, to everyone, on a fairly predictable schedule.

What is worth examining is not whether the intensity went. It is what replaced it.

## Key Takeaways

- Passionate love declines in nearly all long relationships, typically over the first one to three years — its absence is not evidence of a problem.
- What matters is whether it was replaced by companionate love — affection, trust, ease — or by nothing.
- Four states genuinely signal an ending: contempt, relief at their absence, no shared future you want, and having stopped raising things entirely.
- Emptiness is far more recoverable than contempt, and the two are frequently confused.

---

## Two Different Things Called Love

Research on adult relationships has long distinguished between two states, and conflating them causes an enormous amount of unnecessary anxiety.

**Passionate love** is the early phase — intense preoccupation, heightened arousal, the sense of the other person as endlessly interesting. It is chemically distinct, physically demanding, and it does not last. In most relationships it declines substantially over one to three years. This is not a failure of the relationship; sustaining that state indefinitely is not something human beings do.

**Companionate love** is what replaces it — deep affection, trust, ease, genuine liking, the sense of a shared life. It is less dramatic and considerably more durable. Most people describing a long happy marriage are describing this.

The problem is that popular culture depicts only the first and calls it love. So when it fades — on schedule, as it does for everyone — people conclude they have fallen out of love, when what has actually happened is a transition they were never told to expect.

The diagnostic question is therefore not *has the intensity gone*. It is *what is in its place?*

If the answer is warmth, ease, and a person you would still choose, the relationship is doing exactly what long relationships do. If the answer is nothing, that is worth taking seriously.

## What This Is Usually Not

Before the genuine warning signs, four things that commonly get misread as falling out of love.

**Exhaustion.** New parents, people in demanding jobs, anyone in a period of chronic sleep deprivation. Depleted people do not feel much of anything, and the flatness gets attributed to the relationship because that is where it is most noticeable. This resolves when the depletion does.

**Depression.** Anhedonia — the loss of pleasure in things you used to enjoy — is a core feature, and it does not spare relationships. A large number of people who conclude they have stopped loving their partner are describing depression, and the relationship recovers when the depression is treated. Worth ruling out before making any large decision.

**Unspoken resentment.** Accumulated grievances that were never raised do not disappear; they convert into distance. The feeling of numbness is often the sensation of holding several years of unsaid things. This one is very recoverable, and the recovery starts with saying them.

**Boredom, in the ordinary sense.** Two people who stopped being curious about each other. The relationship has not failed; it has stopped moving. Also recoverable, and easier than people expect.

## The Four Signs It Is Actually Over

These are different in kind from the above.

### 1. Contempt

The strongest single predictor of relationship breakdown identified in the research literature.

Contempt is not anger. Anger is engagement — it means something still matters. Contempt is a settled sense of superiority: eye-rolling, mockery, sarcasm about who they fundamentally are, a quiet certainty that you are the more reasonable person.

Anger in a relationship is workable. Contempt is corrosive in a way that rarely reverses, because it changes how you perceive everything the other person does.

### 2. Relief when they are not there

Notice the difference between enjoying solitude and being relieved by their absence.

Everyone benefits from time alone, and wanting it is healthy. What is different is the feeling when they are due back — a small drop, a sense of the free period ending. If their return consistently registers as a loss rather than a gain, that is meaningful information.

### 3. You cannot construct a future you want

Try it concretely. Five years out, in some detail: where you live, what a weekend looks like, what you are doing together.

If nothing comes, or if what comes is something you are enduring rather than choosing, that is significant. People in relationships that have life in them can usually do this easily, even when things are currently hard.

### 4. You have stopped raising things

The quietest sign and the most commonly misread as improvement.

At some point you stopped mentioning the things that bother you. Not because they resolved — because it stopped seeming worth it. From the outside this looks like a relationship with less conflict. It is usually a relationship where one person has finished trying.

Ask yourself when you last raised something difficult. If you cannot remember, ask whether that is because there is nothing to raise or because you concluded it would not change anything.

## Emptiness Versus Contempt

If you take one thing from this: these two states have very different prognoses and they are frequently confused.

**Emptiness** — flat, distant, nothing much happening, neither of you unhappy exactly. This is among the most recoverable relationship problems there is. Two people who stopped being curious can become curious again. It usually requires deliberate work rather than waiting, but the material is still there.

**Contempt** — you have come to hold your partner in low regard as a person. This is a different situation. It can occasionally be reversed with serious work where both people want to, but it is genuinely hard, and pretending otherwise wastes years.

The practical test: can you list five things you genuinely admire about them, right now, without effort? If they come easily, whatever else is wrong, the foundation is intact. If you find yourself qualifying each one, or reaching for things that were true a decade ago, that is worth knowing.

## What to Do Before Deciding

Decisions about ending a long relationship are best not made from inside a flat period, because flat periods distort judgement in a specific direction.

**Rule out the physical.** Sleep, health, medication, depression. This is not a deflection — a substantial proportion of people who present with lost feelings are describing something treatable, and no relationship decision should be made without ruling it out.

**Say the unsaid things.** If there is a list of accumulated grievances you have been carrying, that list is doing more damage than the events on it. Saying them, badly and belatedly, changes more than most people expect.

**Restore some curiosity deliberately.** Ask your partner something you do not already know the answer to. Do something neither of you has done. Novelty has a measurable effect on relationship satisfaction, and the reason it works is not romance — it is that you get to see the person outside the routine you have both memorised.

**Give it a defined period, not an open one.** Three months of genuine effort with a specific plan, then reassess. Open-ended waiting is how people spend five years in a decision they made in year one.

**Get an outside read.** Not from a friend, who will side with you. Someone whose function is to ask what you are avoiding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to fall out of love in a long-term relationship?**
It is normal for the intense early phase to fade — that happens in nearly all long relationships, usually within the first few years. What matters is what replaced it. If there is warmth, trust and ease underneath, the relationship transitioned normally. If there is nothing, that is a different situation and worth examining.

**Can you fall back in love with your partner?**
Frequently, yes, when what is missing is connection rather than regard. Couples who deliberately restore curiosity, novelty and undivided attention often report the feeling returning within months. What does not reliably come back is love lost to contempt, or lost while one partner did all the work alone.

**What is the difference between love and just being comfortable?**
Comfort is part of companionate love, not a substitute for it. The distinguishing question is whether you actively like them — whether you would choose their company over a free evening alone, whether you are still interested in their opinion, whether you would pick them again knowing everything. Comfort without those is habit; comfort with them is a functioning long relationship.

**How do I know if I should leave or try harder?**
Look at what is present rather than what is missing. If the flatness comes with emptiness, trying harder is usually worth it — that is a highly recoverable state. If it comes with contempt, relief at their absence, or no future you want, more effort tends to extend the ending rather than change it. Also rule out depression and exhaustion before deciding either way.

**My partner says they have fallen out of love with me. Is it over?**
Not necessarily, though it depends entirely on what they mean. Some people saying this are describing the normal fading of intensity and have interpreted it as a verdict. Others are describing contempt or a settled decision. Ask them the concrete questions — can they imagine a future, do they still admire you, have they stopped raising things — because the answers distinguish situations that look identical from the outside.

**Can a relationship survive one person falling out of love?**
Sometimes, if the other person is willing to do the work and the first is willing to genuinely try rather than to be persuaded. What does not work is one partner carrying the effort while the other waits to see whether their feelings return. That arrangement exhausts the person trying and rarely changes the outcome.

## The Honest Summary

Most people asking this question are not falling out of love. They are experiencing the ordinary transition from one kind of love to another, and nobody told them it was coming.

A smaller number are describing something real, and the four signs above are the ones that distinguish it.

The difference matters enormously, because one situation calls for patience and deliberate effort, and the other calls for an honest decision. Treating the first as the second ends relationships that were fine. Treating the second as the first costs years.
