# Domestic Violence Law in India: What Protection You Actually Have

Two things stop most people from using the protections that exist. The first is not knowing that non-physical abuse is covered. The second is assuming that seeking protection means filing a criminal case against a spouse, ending the marriage, and leaving the house.

None of that is required. The main protective law in India is a civil remedy, it explicitly covers economic and emotional abuse, and one of the orders it provides is the right to remain in your own home.

**If you are in immediate danger, call 112. The national women's helpline is 181. Both operate 24 hours.**

This is general information, not legal advice. If you are in an unsafe situation, speak to a lawyer or a protection officer — free legal aid is available and how to reach it is set out below.

## Key Takeaways

- The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005 is civil, not criminal — using it does not mean filing a police case or ending your marriage.
- It covers physical, sexual, verbal, emotional and economic abuse, and applies to live-in relationships as well as marriages.
- A residence order can secure your right to stay in the shared household; you do not have to leave to be protected.
- Criminal provisions exist separately under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, and the two routes can run in parallel.
- Free legal aid is available through District Legal Services Authorities regardless of income in domestic violence cases involving women.

## What Counts as Domestic Violence

Section 3 of the Act defines it broadly, and the breadth is the point. It includes:

**Physical abuse** — assault, bodily harm, or anything endangering life, limb or health.

**Sexual abuse** — sexual conduct that abuses, humiliates or degrades, or otherwise violates dignity.

**Verbal and emotional abuse** — insults, ridicule, humiliation, particularly regarding not having a child or not having a male child, and repeated threats causing mental pain.

**Economic abuse** — depriving someone of financial resources they are entitled to, disposing of assets, restricting access to shared household resources or to their own income or streedhan.

**Harassment relating to dowry** — coercion of the woman or her relatives to meet an unlawful demand for property or valuable security.

Economic abuse is the category most often not recognised as abuse at all. Controlling someone's salary, preventing them from working, denying access to household money, or withholding their own jewellery are covered conduct, not domestic friction.

## Who Can Apply, and Against Whom

Any woman in a **domestic relationship** — meaning she lives or has lived in a shared household with the respondent, related by marriage, blood, adoption, or a relationship in the nature of marriage.

This deliberately extends beyond wives. A woman in a live-in relationship is covered. So are sisters, mothers, widows and daughters living in a shared household.

An application can be made against an adult male respondent and, following judicial interpretation, against female relatives of the husband or partner as well — which matters, because in many households the abuse involves in-laws rather than only the spouse.

The Act protects women. Men experiencing abuse from a spouse have recourse under general criminal law and the ordinary civil courts, but not under the PWDVA.

## The Orders a Magistrate Can Pass

This is the practical heart of the Act, and most people are unaware of how much is available.

**Protection order (Section 18)** — prohibits the respondent from committing further violence, entering your workplace or school, contacting you, or attempting to communicate. The most commonly sought relief.

**Residence order (Section 19)** — this one is frequently decisive. It can restrain the respondent from dispossessing you from the shared household, direct him to remove himself from it, restrain him from entering the part you occupy, or require him to provide alternative accommodation.

The key point: **you have a right to reside in the shared household regardless of whether you own it or have any legal interest in it.** Many women leave because they assume they have no claim on a house owned by the husband or his family. That assumption is wrong, and leaving frequently weakens their position.

**Monetary relief (Section 20)** — compensation for losses including medical expenses, loss of earnings, damage to property, and maintenance for you and your children. This can run alongside maintenance claims under other laws.

**Custody order (Section 21)** — temporary custody of children.

**Compensation order (Section 22)** — for mental torture and emotional distress.

**Interim and ex parte orders (Section 23)** — a magistrate can grant relief immediately, including without hearing the respondent first, where the situation warrants it. This matters when the risk is present rather than theoretical.

## How the Process Actually Runs

**Step 1 — The complaint.** An application under Section 12 goes to the Judicial Magistrate First Class. It can be filed by you, by a Protection Officer, or by a registered service provider on your behalf.

**Step 2 — The Domestic Incident Report.** A Protection Officer records the details of the incidents. Every district has appointed Protection Officers, and their role includes assisting with the application, arranging medical examination, and helping you access shelter and legal aid.

**Step 3 — Notice and hearing.** The respondent is served notice. The Act contemplates the first hearing within three days of the application, and disposal within sixty days — timelines that are frequently exceeded in practice, though interim orders can be obtained early.

**Step 4 — Orders.** The magistrate passes such orders as the situation requires, and they are enforceable.

**Step 5 — Breach.** Breaching a protection order is a criminal offence under Section 31, punishable with imprisonment up to one year, a fine, or both. This is what gives the civil order practical teeth.

## The Criminal Route Is Separate

The PWDVA is civil. There is a separate criminal provision for cruelty by a husband or his relatives.

Under the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023**, which replaced the Indian Penal Code from July 2024, this is now **Section 85** (cruelty by husband or relative of husband), with Section 86 defining cruelty. It corresponds to the long-familiar Section 498A IPC. Cruelty here includes conduct likely to drive a woman to suicide or cause grave injury, and harassment to coerce an unlawful dowry demand.

The two routes can run in parallel — a civil protection order and a criminal complaint are not alternatives.

Worth understanding before choosing: the criminal route can result in arrest and prosecution of the husband and named relatives. It is the appropriate response to serious cruelty. It also generally forecloses reconciliation and tends to escalate matters with the extended family. Many women want protection and financial security without initiating a prosecution, and the PWDVA is designed for exactly that.

Which route fits depends on your situation and your objectives, and it is worth discussing with a lawyer before filing anything.

## Where to Get Help

**Emergency — call 112.**

**Women Helpline — 181.** National, 24 hours, provides crisis support and referral.

**National Commission for Women** operates a complaint mechanism and a WhatsApp helpline for women in distress.

**Protection Officers** are appointed in every district under the Act. The District Women and Child Development Office can direct you to yours.

**Free legal aid** is available through **District Legal Services Authorities (DLSA)** in every district, and through the **National Legal Services Authority helpline, 15100**. Under the Legal Services Authorities Act, women are entitled to free legal aid, and this is not means-tested for them. This is the most underused resource in the system — many people assume they cannot afford a lawyer and do not know that one is available at no cost.

**One Stop Centres (Sakhi Centres)** operate in most districts, providing medical aid, police assistance, legal aid, psychological support and temporary shelter in a single location.

## What to Do Before You Act

If you are planning to seek protection, a few practical steps materially improve your position.

**Document.** Dates, incidents, what was said and done. Photographs of injuries. Medical records — attend a doctor even for minor injuries and ensure the cause is recorded. Screenshots of threatening messages. Keep copies somewhere the respondent cannot access, ideally with a trusted person or in a cloud account with a password he does not know.

**Secure your documents.** Aadhaar, PAN, passport, bank papers, educational certificates, marriage certificate, children's documents. Originals or copies, kept outside the house if possible.

**Open an account he cannot access,** if you can do so safely.

**Tell one person outside the household** what is happening. Isolation is both a common feature of abusive relationships and a significant practical vulnerability.

**Do not leave the shared household without advice** unless you are in immediate danger — in which case leave, and sort the legal position afterwards. Where there is no immediate danger, take advice first, because leaving voluntarily can complicate a later residence claim.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does domestic violence have to be physical?**
No. The Act expressly covers verbal, emotional, sexual and economic abuse. Sustained humiliation, threats, controlling access to money, preventing someone from working, or withholding their streedhan are all covered conduct. The absence of physical violence does not put a situation outside the law.

**Can I get protection without filing a criminal case?**
Yes, and this is one of the most useful features of the Act. Proceedings under the PWDVA are civil. You can obtain protection orders, residence orders and monetary relief without a police case and without your husband being arrested. A criminal complaint under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita is a separate and optional route.

**Do I have to leave the house if I report domestic violence?**
No. A residence order under Section 19 can secure your right to remain in the shared household, and the magistrate can direct the respondent to leave it instead. Your right to reside does not depend on owning the property or on having any legal interest in it. Many women leave believing they have no claim, which is both incorrect and often against their interests.

**Does the Domestic Violence Act apply to live-in relationships?**
Yes. The Act covers a woman in a relationship in the nature of marriage, and courts have applied it to live-in relationships that meet that description. It also covers other women in the household, including sisters, mothers and widows.

**Can my in-laws be named in a domestic violence case?**
Yes. Applications can be made against female relatives of the husband as well as against him, which matters because in many households the abuse involves in-laws. Both the civil provisions and the criminal cruelty provision extend to relatives of the husband.

**How much does it cost to file a domestic violence case?**
Very little, and potentially nothing. Court fees are minimal, and free legal aid is available to women through District Legal Services Authorities without a means test. A Protection Officer will also assist with preparing and filing the application. Cost should not be the reason anyone stays in an unsafe situation.

## One Thing Worth Saying Plainly

Couples counselling is not the right response to abuse, and it can increase risk. Joint sessions require both people to speak honestly in front of each other, and in a relationship involving coercion that exposes one person to retaliation for what they disclose.

Where there is abuse, the appropriate support is individual — a therapist experienced in coercive control, a Protection Officer, a lawyer. Not couples work, and not mediation with the person causing the harm.
