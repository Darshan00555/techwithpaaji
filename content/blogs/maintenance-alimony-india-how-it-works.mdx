# Maintenance and Alimony in India: How Courts Actually Decide

The first thing worth saying is that there is no formula. People search for an alimony calculator and find figures presented with a confidence the law does not actually support.

Indian courts decide maintenance on a set of factors, weighed case by case. Two couples with identical incomes can receive materially different orders depending on the length of the marriage, the standard of living, what each person gave up, and what each can realistically earn now.

This explains how that assessment works. It is general information rather than legal advice, and the outcome in your case will turn on facts a lawyer needs to see.

## Key Takeaways

- There is no statutory formula for alimony in India — courts weigh a list of factors and exercise discretion.
- Maintenance can be claimed under several laws at once, and which route you use affects speed, quantum and enforcement.
- The Supreme Court's 2020 judgment in Rajnesh v. Neha made an affidavit of assets and liabilities mandatory and standardised the criteria.
- The commonly quoted "25% of net income" comes from a specific case and functions as a benchmark, not a rule.
- Maintenance for a spouse and maintenance for children are separate obligations, assessed separately.

---

## Two Different Things: Maintenance and Alimony

These get used interchangeably and they are not the same.

**Maintenance** is ongoing financial support, usually monthly. It can be claimed during proceedings (interim) or after them (permanent), and it can be sought even where no divorce is being pursued.

**Alimony**, in common Indian usage, generally refers to a one-time lump sum settlement at the point of divorce, in place of or alongside monthly payments.

Courts award either or both. A lump sum has the advantage of finality and removes years of enforcement problems. Monthly maintenance protects against a lump sum being spent or mismanaged, and can be revised if circumstances change materially.

## Which Law You Claim Under

This matters more than most people realise, because the routes differ in speed and scope.

**Section 144, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita 2023** — the provision that replaced the long-familiar Section 125 of the CrPC from July 2024. It is secular, applies regardless of religion, and covers a wife, children and parents unable to maintain themselves. It is generally the fastest route and is frequently used first.

**Sections 24 and 25, Hindu Marriage Act 1955** — Section 24 covers maintenance during proceedings, Section 25 covers permanent alimony at or after the decree. Both are worded gender-neutrally, so a husband may claim under them.

**Section 18, Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act 1956** — allows a Hindu wife to claim maintenance from her husband without seeking divorce, including where she is living separately for justified reasons.

**Section 20, Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005** — monetary relief, which can include maintenance, obtained through a proceeding that is often quicker than a full matrimonial suit.

**Special Marriage Act 1954, Indian Divorce Act 1869**, and the relevant provisions of Muslim, Parsi and Christian personal law apply to marriages under those regimes.

Claims under more than one law can run simultaneously. Courts adjust for amounts already awarded so there is no double recovery, and the Supreme Court has directed that any existing orders must be disclosed.

## What Courts Actually Weigh

In **Rajnesh v. Neha (2020)**, the Supreme Court set out criteria to bring consistency to what had been a widely inconsistent area. The factors include:

- **Status of the parties** — the social and financial standing of both.
- **Reasonable needs** of the person claiming, and of any dependent children.
- **Qualifications and employment status** of both parties.
- **Independent income or assets** held by the applicant.
- **Standard of living enjoyed during the marriage** — a significant factor, and the reason a long marriage to a high earner produces different outcomes from a short one.
- **Sacrifices made for family responsibilities** — a spouse who left employment to raise children or care for in-laws is not penalised for the resulting gap in earnings.
- **Litigation costs** for a non-working spouse.
- **The paying spouse's income, assets, and their own liabilities and obligations.**

Length of the marriage runs through all of it. A twenty-year marriage where one partner never worked produces a very different assessment from a two-year marriage between two earning professionals.

---

## The Rajnesh v. Neha Procedure

That judgment also changed how these claims are run, and the practical points matter:

**An affidavit of assets and liabilities is mandatory** from both parties in every maintenance proceeding across the country. This was the central reform. It substantially reduced the previous pattern of income being concealed or understated, since a false affidavit carries consequences.

**Maintenance is ordinarily payable from the date of the application**, not the date of the order. Given that these proceedings can run for years, this is financially significant.

**Existing orders must be disclosed** so overlapping claims can be adjusted.

**Enforcement was strengthened**, including recovery as arrears of land revenue and attachment of property, addressing the long-standing problem of orders that were won and never paid.

## About the 25% Figure

You will see this quoted everywhere as though it were settled law. It is not.

It derives largely from **Kalyan Dey Chowdhury v. Rita Dey Chowdhury (2017)**, where the Supreme Court observed that 25% of the husband's net salary would be a just and proper figure in the circumstances of that case.

It has since become a widely used reference point, and many practitioners will tell you orders often land somewhere near it. But it is a benchmark drawn from one set of facts, not a rule courts are bound to apply. Awards routinely fall above and below it depending on the factors above — particularly where there are children, where the marriage was long, or where the claiming spouse has no earning capacity.

Treat it as a rough anchor for expectations, not a calculation.

## Maintenance for Children Is Separate

This is frequently misunderstood, and it causes real problems in settlements.

A parent's obligation to maintain a child is independent of what is agreed between the spouses. A wife may waive her own maintenance in a settlement; she cannot waive the child's, because it is not hers to give up.

Child maintenance covers reasonable needs including education, medical expenses and a standard of living broadly consistent with the parents' means. It generally continues until the child attains majority, and courts have extended it further for a child unable to maintain themselves, including where they are still in education or have a disability.

It is also revisable. As school fees rise and needs change, either parent can apply for modification. Settlements that fix a child maintenance figure permanently with no provision for revision tend to return to court.

## Can a Husband Claim Maintenance?

Under Sections 24 and 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act, yes — the provisions are gender-neutral, and courts have granted maintenance to husbands where the wife has substantially greater means and the husband genuinely cannot maintain himself.

In practice such awards are uncommon, and courts scrutinise them closely. A husband who is able-bodied and employable but not working will generally not succeed simply because his wife earns more. The threshold in practice is genuine inability rather than disparity in income.

Under Section 144 BNSS, a husband cannot claim — that provision covers a wife, children and parents.

## What Actually Changes the Outcome

Three things influence the result more than most people expect, and all three are within your control.

**Documentation.** Salary slips, bank statements, tax returns, loan records, evidence of standard of living. Maintenance proceedings are substantially documentary, and the party with clearer records tends to fare better. This includes the paying spouse, whose genuine liabilities count only if they can be evidenced.

**Honesty in the affidavit.** Concealment used to be routine and is now considerably more dangerous. Discovered concealment damages credibility on every other issue in the case.

**Whether you settle.** A negotiated figure, arrived at with advice on both sides, almost always beats a litigated one on time and cost. Contested maintenance can run for years, and the legal fees frequently consume a meaningful fraction of what is being fought over.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is there a fixed alimony calculator for India?**
No. Despite the number of online calculators, Indian law prescribes no formula. Courts weigh the factors set out in Rajnesh v. Neha and exercise discretion. Any calculator you find is applying an assumed percentage that has no statutory basis, and relying on it to plan a settlement is a mistake.

**Can a working wife claim maintenance?**
Yes. Employment does not bar a claim. What courts examine is whether her income is sufficient to maintain the standard of living enjoyed during the marriage. A significant disparity in earnings can still support an award, though the amount will reflect her own income. A wife earning comfortably at a level comparable to her husband is considerably less likely to receive an order.

**How long does it take to get a maintenance order?**
Interim maintenance is meant to be decided quickly, and the Supreme Court has directed that applications be disposed of within a reasonable period — commonly targeted at sixty days. In practice it varies substantially by court and workload. Final maintenance is decided with the main proceeding and can take considerably longer.

**What happens if my spouse refuses to pay a maintenance order?**
Non-payment is enforceable. Remedies include recovery as arrears of land revenue, attachment of property and salary, and in persistent cases, imprisonment for non-compliance. Rajnesh v. Neha specifically strengthened enforcement because unpaid orders had become a widespread problem. Keep records of every payment made or missed, since enforcement proceedings turn on that record.

**Can maintenance be changed after it is ordered?**
Yes, on a material change in circumstances — job loss, serious illness, a substantial change in income, or a change in the child's needs. Either party can apply. Maintenance is not a permanently fixed figure, though a court will expect the change to be genuine and evidenced rather than convenient.

**Does maintenance stop if my ex-spouse remarries?**
A wife's right to maintenance generally ends on remarriage, and the paying spouse can apply to have the order cancelled. Child maintenance does not end — the biological parent's obligation continues regardless of either parent's remarriage, since it is owed to the child rather than to the former spouse.

## A Practical Note

The most damaging thing that happens in maintenance disputes is that they become the arena for everything else — the anger about the marriage, the sense of being wronged, the wish for the other person to be held accountable. That is understandable, and it is expensive. Cases fought that way run for years and both people lose.

If you can separate the financial question from the emotional one, you will settle faster and keep more of what you are arguing over.
